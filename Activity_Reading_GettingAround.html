Why do some people never get lost?

Adapted from ScienceABC — "Why Do Some People Have A Better Sense Of Direction Than Others?" · read the original ↗

Some people can find their way around a new city after one visit. Others need GPS to reach a shop around the corner. So why are some of us "super-navigators" while the rest of us get lost so easily?

Scientists call a good sense of direction wayfinding — knowing where you are and how to reach your destination without a map or phone. There are two main strategies. Some people rely on route knowledge: a memorised chain of turns and landmarks, such as "left at the bakery, right after the church". Others build survey knowledge: a mental bird's-eye map of how everything fits together. Survey navigators can usually invent a shortcut and still know which way home is.

Inside the brain, three types of cell do the work. Place cells, discovered by Nobel Prize winner John O'Keefe, tell us where we are. Grid cells map where we have been, and head-direction cells act like a compass. Some of this ability may be there from birth, but most of it is learned.

That is why experts describe navigation as a "use-it-or-lose-it" skill. Leaning on GPS for every journey can weaken it, because we stop noticing landmarks and building mental maps. Anxiety doesn't help either: if people are told they are hopeless at directions, they often start to believe it.

The good news? Wayfinding improves with practice. Researchers suggest switching off the GPS sometimes, paying attention to landmarks and the direction you set off in, glancing back over your shoulder, and using a full map instead of turn-by-turn instructions — so your brain has to do the work itself.

Getting around & directions

Real phrases for finding your way, with MFP: Meaning · Form · Pronunciation. (Use these to role-play in the city!)

🙋 Asking for directions

Excuse me, how do I get to …?
Is there a … near here?
Is it far? / How long does it take?
M ask the way politely.  F how do I get to + place.  P weak to /tə/; start with "Excuse me".

➡️ Giving directions

Go straight on.
Turn left / right at …
It's on your left, opposite the …
M tell someone the way.  F imperatives: Go / Turn / Take + …  P stress the direction: LEFT, RIGHT.

🚇 Public transport

Which line / bus goes to …?
Where do I change?
Does this train stop at …?
M use the network.  F Which … goes to + place?  P Which /wɪtʃ/; rising tone on the question.

🔁 Checking & clarifying

Sorry, could you repeat that?
So it's the second left?
Did you say left or right?
M make sure you understood.  F So it's … ? (confirm).  P fall–rise = polite checking.
